The source is a 2008 EV Grieve blog post (itself quoting an August 1953 *Mechanix Illustrated* feature on failed inventions) describing **talking chairs installed at New York's Hotel Edison** (228 West 47th Street, Midtown Manhattan) ([EV Grieve](https://evgrieve.com/2008/08/happens-to-best-of-us.html)). **The mechanism, and the bit**: a guest's own body weight sitting in the chair triggered a lever that started a hidden phonograph record — the hotel's vice president, Irwin Kramer, had pitched it as a direct, built-in advertising channel, presumably a recorded welcome message touting the hotel's features. **The actual payoff**: at least one guest was reportedly so startled by a disembodied voice suddenly speaking from the furniture that she ran into the hallway insisting there was a man in her room. The hotel pulled the chairs shortly after. That's the whole bit in one image: decades before anyone worried about smart-speaker privacy or in-room voice assistants, a hotel already tried wiring a guest's own chair to talk to them — and it went about as well as it would today. **Honest limitation, stated rather than papered over — and it's a real one**: this entire anecdote traces to a single secondary source (the 2008 EV Grieve blog post), itself relaying a single 1953 magazine article that hasn't been independently located or cross-checked this round. Unlike the minibar or teleseme briefs, there's no second corroborating outlet, no numbers (how many chairs, how long they ran, how many guests encountered them), and no confirmation beyond "the hotel removed them" — `story-researcher` should treat finding the original *Mechanix Illustrated* piece (or any second source) as a precondition for scripting, not a nice-to-have, and should be prepared to walk away from the idea if it can't be corroborated. **Structural note for `scriptwriter`**: this doesn't fit the Rise-of-X template cleanly — there's no decades-long arc, no rise-and-decline shape, just a single invention that flopped almost immediately — so it reads better as a short, self-contained "hotel history's weirdest flops" bit (with room to expand into a recurring short-format bit if more examples turn up) than a full mock-serious deep-dive. Asset fit: moderate — no named-collaboration-brand tie, and thinner editorial-insider weight than most of this project's history pieces since the anecdote itself is so brief, but it's squarely inside the mundane-hotel-detail comedic lane CLAUDE.md already validates, applied to hotel technology rather than hotel objects. Hook: hotels have been trying to make the room talk to you for over 70 years, and the very first attempt ended with a guest convinced a man was hiding in her chair.
