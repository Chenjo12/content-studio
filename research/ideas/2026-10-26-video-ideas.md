## Ideas

1. **"Savannah's Oldest Hotel Was a Civil War Hospital Twice Over — and a Yellow Fever Ward Twice More. The Ghost Tours Are the Least Interesting Part."** A genuinely new hotel-history candidate, confirmed via grep as untouched across all 76 prior batches and `research/production-queue.csv` (no hits for "Marshall House," "Savannah," or "yellow fever" anywhere in the project before today). **The real, documented history**: Savannah's Marshall House (built 1851 by Mary Marshall, on Broughton Street) was occupied by Union troops during General Sherman's 1864–65 occupation of the city and used as a hospital for wounded soldiers until the war's end — corroborated independently across the hotel's own history page, a Savannah hotel-history writeup, and general Civil War-Savannah sourcing, not just one promotional source ([Marshall House](https://marshallhouse.com/history/), [UniqHotels](https://www.uniqhotels.com/the-marshall-house/)). Separately, the building served as a hospital again during Savannah's 19th-century yellow fever epidemics — epidemics that are independently, rigorously documented at the city level by historian Tim Lockley (University of Warwick): **650 dead in the 1854 epidemic** (293 of them Irish immigrants, with Savannah's Black population — nearly half the city — accounting for just 14 of the deaths, a stark and citable mortality disparity) and **1,066 dead in the 1876 epidemic**, which began with a single infected sailor in July and emptied roughly a fifth of the city within six weeks ([Wikipedia: 1876 yellow fever epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1876_yellow_fever_epidemic), [WAMC/Warwick](https://wamc.org/academic-minute/2012-10-15/dr-tim-lockley-university-of-warwick-savannah-and-yellow-fever)). **The editorial angle, and why this isn't just another haunted-hotel piece** (a category this project has correctly declined five times running — 2026-10-09, 10-14, 10-22, 10-24, 10-25 — for thin, legend-grade sourcing): the Marshall House is marketed almost exclusively through ghost-tour framing, but the real history underneath — a building that repeatedly became a public-health and wartime-casualty site precisely because it was the kind of large, central, easily-requisitioned building a hotel is — is the more rigorous and more gripping story, and it's hiding in plain sight behind a worse one. **Honest limitation, stated rather than papered over**: no source gives an exact death count *inside the Marshall House building itself* — only citywide epidemic totals (well-sourced) and the building's documented role as one of several requisitioned hospital sites (also well-sourced, independently of ghost-tour material). The widely repeated claim that a 1990s renovation uncovered human remains/amputated limbs beneath the floorboards is contested — some tourism sources state it as fact, while at least one forum-level check found no primary source behind it — `story-researcher` should treat this specific detail as unverified folklore and either independently confirm it against a primary record (renovation permits, a contemporary news report) or drop it entirely rather than let the strongest visual beat in the story rest on an unconfirmed claim. Exact yellow-fever-hospital dates at this specific building also aren't pinned to precise years in the sourcing found today. Source: today's pulse-check. Platform(s): YouTube (video essay — the two-epidemic, two-war structure needs room to breathe, and this is exactly the hotel-history category CLAUDE.md names Harbin-fire/Taj Mumbai/Ritz Paris-style gravity for), Bilibili (same documented-history/knowledge-content lane trend-scout keeps flagging as an open gap), XHS (shorter cut only if the gripping register still lands in that format — flag to `scriptwriter` to judge, since this is heavier than XHS's usual register). Asset fit: moderate — no named-collaboration-brand tie (consistent with the Manila Hotel and Ritz Paris precedents already in the production queue, where this project treats hotel-history as its own valid category independent of brand-collaboration credentials) — but it's squarely the hotel-history deep-dive category CLAUDE.md names as a standing priority, and draws on real historical-research rigor as its own form of editorial credibility. Hook: the building everyone visits for its ghost tour was, twice, an actual hospital for the dying — the ghosts are the marketing; the epidemics and the war are the parts nobody's telling you about.
