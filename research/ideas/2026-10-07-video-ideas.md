## Ideas

1. **"Rise of the Peephole."** The next entry in the mock-serious Speeed-style object-history format (minibar, Do Not Disturb sign, wake-up call, room safe, bellhop, ice machine, room key, luggage sticker, presidential suite) — and a genuinely unused subject, confirmed via grep across all 57 prior batches and the production queue before proposing it. **The contested-origin cold open, same shape that made the minibar brief work**: the common nickname for a door peephole — "Judas hole" / "Judas window" — has a murky, debated etymology; one account ties it to an old painting of guards watching Judas through a hole, another to church confession-booth peepholes priests used, and nobody can actually pin down which is real ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peephole), [Straight Dope](https://boards.straightdope.com/t/judas-window/710524)). The device itself traces to medieval castle door slits for assessing threats before opening up, formalized into the modern one-way fisheye peephole patented and widely adopted in early-20th-century apartment and hotel doors as a security feature. **The absurd mid-story twist**: the "one-way" privacy promise is trivially reversible — a cheap "peephole reverser" or "tactical door viewer" device (looks like a small telescope) lets someone standing in the hallway see *into* the room, not just the reverse. This isn't theoretical: ESPN reporter Erin Andrews was illegally filmed through a hotel peephole rigged by a stalker, and in 2024 a traveler's viral TikTok showed a Bellagio Las Vegas peephole installed backwards, letting anyone in the hallway look straight into her room (("inside hotel room peephole tiktok", [Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/?p=1190678)); background on the exploit mechanism via [SmarterTravel](https://www.smartertravel.com/hotel-room-security-feature-that-can-be-turned-against-you/)). **The quiet ending**: hotels are now phasing the peephole out entirely, not fixing it — mechanical peepholes are being replaced across the industry by electronic keyless-entry systems (RFID, Bluetooth, biometric) as part of a broader contactless push, and a few flagship properties (e.g. Grand Hyatt DFW) have swapped the glass fisheye for a small camera screen instead — though adoption of that specific fix is rare because of battery/maintenance cost, so most hotels are just quietly dropping the feature as new doors get specified rather than solving it. A hundred-year-old security feature nobody can agree got its creepy nickname honestly, that turned out to make rooms *less* private than guests assumed, is now being retired by accident rather than by design — the same "sources disagree, then the thing just dies" arc the minibar brief proved works. Source: channel-analyst: speeed (mock-serious object-history format) / today's pulse-check for a fresh, unused Rise-of-X subject. Platform(s): YouTube (the format's proven home), XHS as a shorter cut (the "the hotel 'privacy' feature that's secretly reversible" hook is clean and save-worthy on its own). Asset fit: moderate — no named collaboration-brand tie, but explaining *why* the exploit works (fisheye optics, the physics of why a reverser defeats them) and reading the quiet-phase-out as an access-control/liability decision rather than a design refresh is real hospitality-operations literacy a generalist travel account wouldn't think to question. Register: comedic (same register as the minibar, room-key, and presidential-suite entries) — the security-exploit beat needs a straight-faced "here's how easily this fails" delivery, not alarmist framing; this is a quirk to be amused by, not a safety PSA, so it should land closer to the minibar's tone than to a true-crime framing. Hook: hotels sold you a hundred years of "nobody can see in" — and the thing that promise was built on turns out to work both ways with a $15 gadget, which is probably why they're replacing it instead of fixing it.
