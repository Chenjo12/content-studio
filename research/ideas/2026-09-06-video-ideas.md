1. **"The Hotel Lobby That Was Designed to Be Iconic Became the Deadliest Structural Collapse in US History Until 9/11" — the Hyatt Regency Kansas City walkway collapse.** On July 17, 1981, two suspended pedestrian walkways crossing the atrium lobby of the Hyatt Regency Crown Center in Kansas City collapsed onto a crowded tea-dance competition below, killing 114 people and injuring over 200 — at the time, the single deadliest structural failure in US history. The cause wasn't an act of god: a last-minute fabrication-driven change to the hanger-rod design (switching from a single rod running through both walkways to two separate rods) doubled the load on the upper walkway's connection point, leaving it able to bear only an estimated 30% of the code-mandated minimum — and the change was never re-checked by the engineer of record ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Hyatt-Regency-walkway-collapse), [ASCE](https://www.asce.org/publications-and-news/civil-engineering-source/civil-engineering-magazine/article/2007/01/the-hyatt-regency-walkway-collapse), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyatt_Regency_walkway_collapse)). The hospitality-insider layer most retellings (which treat this purely as an engineering-ethics case study) skip entirely: the atrium itself — a soaring, multi-story open lobby with suspended walkways as a dramatic architectural centerpiece — was a specific, fashionable hotel-design trend of that exact era (popularized by architect John Portman's atrium hotels), meant to make an arriving guest feel the building's scale and ambition the moment they walked in. The disaster happened inside the part of the hotel engineered specifically to impress. One more fact worth including for accuracy: the property quietly rebranded away from the Hyatt name entirely, becoming the Sheraton Kansas City Hotel at Crown Center in 2012 ([Meetings & Conventions](https://www.meetings-conventions.com/News/Hotels-and-Resorts/Hyatt-Regency-in-Kansas-City-to-Become-a-Sheraton)) — worth story-researcher confirming current details, but a real, on-brand detail about how a hotel brand distances itself from a catastrophe attached to its name. Asset fit: strong — explaining *why* atrium lobbies were being built that dramatic in the first place is genuine hospitality/architectural-design literacy, not true-crime narration. Register: **gripping-dramatic — hotel history category, real deaths, needs the same extra-rigor sourcing pass CLAUDE.md already holds the Ritz Paris and Manila Hotel entries to.** Hook: the walkways collapsed in the one part of the hotel that was built to make you say "wow" the second you walked in — and nobody who built it checked the one number that would have stopped it.

2. **"The Hotel Room Didn't Always Come With Its Own Bathroom — One Man's Plumbing Trick Made It Standard."** In 1908, Ellsworth Milton Statler opened the Buffalo Statler with 300 guest rooms and 300 private bathrooms — the first hotel where every room had its own bath, at a time when even upscale hotels used shared facilities down the hall and charged 25 cents extra for hot water. The mechanism that made it financially possible, not just a design choice: Statler invented the "Statler plumbing shaft," which let bathrooms be built back-to-back sharing one set of water, waste, heating, and electrical lines — cutting the cost of "two baths" down to barely more than one — and the same shaft design is still used in hotel and apartment construction today. His slogan, "A room and a bath for a dollar and a half," turned a luxury into an expectation almost overnight ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4067846/nobody-asked-me-butno-132-hotel-history-ellsworth-milton-statler), [Historic Hotels](https://www.historichotels.org/us/hotels-resorts/the-statler/history.php), [Statler Foundation](https://statlerfoundation.com/statler-history/)). Statler's other granular firsts from the same hotel — ice water piped to every bathroom, a phone in every room, a full closet with its own light, a towel hook by the mirror, a free morning newspaper, a pin-cushion with needle and thread — are a genuine treasure trove of "wait, someone had to invent that" mock-serious beats for the same video. Source: today's pulse-check. Platform(s): YouTube (the Rise-of-X format's proven home), XHS as a shorter cut. Asset fit: strong — this is real hospitality-history literacy (an actual named industry pioneer and the specific engineering trick behind an amenity every guest now takes for granted), not trivia. Register: comedic — mock-serious Speeed-style "Rise of [Object]" treatment, same register as the minibar and ice machine entries. **Note: this would be the Rise-of-X format's 9th entry** (after minibar, Do Not Disturb sign, wake-up call, room safe, bellhop, ice machine, room key, luggage sticker) — the last two batches have flagged the growing count as a standing question for the creator (open-ended series vs. a cap) without an answer yet; including this one anyway because the mechanism (a specific plumbing-engineering trick, not just an object's timeline) is genuinely distinct from all 8 prior entries, not a repeat of the pattern. Hook: hotels didn't add private bathrooms because guests demanded them — one hotelier figured out how to plumb two bathrooms for the price of 1.1, and an entire industry standard was born from a pipe-routing trick.
