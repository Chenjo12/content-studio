1. **"A Hotel Room Fire Killed 88 People in Seoul — and the Next Day's Headlines Blamed the Dancers, Not the Locked Doors."** A genuinely new history candidate, confirmed via grep as untouched across all 84 prior batches and `research/production-queue.csv` (no hits for "Daewang," "Brown Hotel," "Time Club," "Dongdaemun," or "Seoul fire" anywhere in the project before today). **The anniversary is exact, not approximate**: the fire broke out at roughly 2:47–2:48 a.m. on **November 3, 1974** — 52 years ago to the day as of this batch — in the **Daewang Corner building**, an eight-story mixed-use complex in Dongdaemun, Seoul, that held restaurants, a department store, banks, offices, apartments, the **Brown Hotel**, and the **Time Go-Go nightclub**, all in one structure ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daewang_Corner_fire)). **The hotel is not incidental to the story — it's where the fire started**: a short circuit ignited in a sixth-floor room of the Brown Hotel, directly adjacent to the nightclub floor; one source places it specifically in room 618. The death toll is reported at **88, with 35 injured**, including seven of the nightclub's own employees — making it, at the time, one of the deadliest fires in Korean history ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daewang_Corner_fire), [Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/amp/southkorea/globalcommunity/20230616/korea-encounters-blaming-victims-of-1974-go-go-club-fire)). **The real hospitality-insider mechanism underneath the tragedy, which is the actual pitch for this creator specifically**: this wasn't a freak accident in a building with no warning — the same building had already burned once before, on August 5, 1972, killing six people and injuring over 80 ([Korea Times]). And the death toll in 1974 was made worse by a documented operational failure, not just fire spread: **employees locked the doors to stop people from leaving without paying**, trapping guests and dancers on the floor as the fire spread ([Korea Times]). A hospitality-management-trained host can name exactly what failed here in terms a tabloid retelling never will: fire egress design, exit-door policy, and the gap between a building's legal capacity/mixed-use licensing and what was actually being run inside it. **The real editorial sting, and the reason this isn't just a disaster recap**: Korea Times' own 2023 retrospective ("Blaming victims of 1974 go-go club fire") documents that contemporary 1974 press coverage largely blamed the *victims* — twisting the fact that dancers kept going because they didn't realize the building was on fire into a mocking headline along the lines of "Even if I burn to death, I'm going to dance" — rather than the locked doors, the building's second fire in two years, or the nightclub's documented illegal operation (licensed as foreigners-only, it was in practice serving Korean patrons past its legal curfew). That's a real, documented case of a hospitality/entertainment-venue disaster getting reframed in the press as a morality story about the victims' behavior instead of an operations and enforcement failure — exactly the kind of insider reframe this creator's training supports, and a stronger story than a straight "forgotten disaster" recap. **Honest limitations, stated rather than papered over — unusually important here given real named deaths**: the death toll is inconsistent across sources — 88 is the figure given by the dedicated Wikipedia article and contemporaneous reporting, but a separate Wikipedia list ("List of man-made disasters in South Korea") gives 64 for the same event, which appears to be an error but hasn't been independently reconciled this round. Start time (2:47 a.m. vs. 2:48 a.m.) and the exact room number (618) also vary slightly by source. All sourcing found this round is English-language (Wikipedia, Korea Times, a crowd-safety blog) — none of it is a primary Korean government or fire-department record, and `story-researcher` must not state the 88 figure, the room number, or the "locked doors" detail as settled fact on camera without attempting to verify against a primary Korean-language source (Seoul Fire Department archives, contemporary Korean newspaper archives) first. Given CLAUDE.md's explicit rule that real tragedies need extra rigor and respect, this should be treated as a research-priority item, not a quick turnaround. Source: today's pulse-check.
