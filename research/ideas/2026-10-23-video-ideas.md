## Ideas

1. **"Rise of the Hotel Matchbook — The Free Souvenir That Was Also an Ashtray Full of Advertising."** A genuinely new Rise-of-X candidate, confirmed via grep as untouched across all 73 prior batches and `research/production-queue.csv` ("matchbook" was searched and logged as a near-miss on 2026-10-13, but never actually proposed as its own idea until today). **The rise**: hotel matchbook production peaked at roughly 500,000 units in 1945, with hotels among the very first businesses — alongside restaurants and airlines — to adopt matchbook covers as a pocket-sized advertising medium; the 1940s-50s are widely called the format's golden age ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchbook)). A hotel's matchbook was simultaneously a free amenity, a tiny billboard a guest carried into the world for free, and — per the surviving collector market — sometimes a genuine historical artifact: Wikipedia notes a 1927 Lindbergh-dinner matchbook sold at auction for $6,000 in 2015. **The fall, and the actual mechanism worth explaining, not just stating**: disposable lighters chipped away at utility through the 1970s-80s, but the real killer was the wave of U.S. smoking bans from the late 1980s onward — once lighting a cigarette indoors stopped being normal, the bowl of free matchbooks by the hotel lobby ashtray stopped making sense as a line item, and one 2011 report specifically documents bars losing their matchbook bowls after a local smoking ban took effect. **The afterlife, which is the funniest part**: the hobby of collecting them has an actual name — phillumeny, its practitioners phillumenists — and hotel matchbooks remain a popular target precisely because, unlike coins or stamps, they were given away by the millions and are now disappearing fast enough to feel like a genuine "endangered species" within a hobbyist's own lifetime ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchbook)). **Honest limitation, stated rather than papered over**: the exact year the decline "began" varies by source (some say late 1980s, one trade source says mid-1980s/early 1990s), and there's no single comprehensive history of hotel matchbooks specifically, as distinct from matchbooks generally — `story-researcher` should treat the hotel-specific angle (as opposed to the broader restaurant/airline matchbook story) as the thing to dig deeper on, not assume the general Wikipedia history transfers one-to-one. Source: today's pulse-check. Platform(s): YouTube (the Rise-of-X format's proven home), Bilibili (same hospitality-history/knowledge-content lane as the minibar and teleseme pieces), XHS (a shorter cut built around "there's a word for people who collect these" as the standalone hook). Asset fit: moderate — no named-collaboration-brand tie, but this is squarely the mock-serious object-history template CLAUDE.md explicitly flags as proven and repeatable, and reading matchbook economics as a real line-item-cost story (not just a nostalgia list) is the same insider-mechanism framing the minibar and teleseme briefs already used well. Register: **comedic — Rise-of-X.** Nothing here calls for gravity. Hook: hotels used to hand you a tiny cardboard advertisement every single time you checked in, for free, for decades — and the thing that finally killed it wasn't a competitor, it was the surgeon general.

2. **"Hotels Have a Word for Sending You to a Worse Hotel, and It's Not 'Sorry.'"** An insider-vocabulary mechanism-reveal, in the same lane as the already-well-received Forbes Five-Star Inspector and AHLA-cost pieces in the production queue, but built around hospitality jargon itself rather than a single process. Confirmed genuinely new via grep — no prior batch has proposed a hotel-jargon/vocabulary piece. **The core mechanism**: when a hotel oversells itself (a deliberate, routine revenue-management practice — hotels oversell rooms on purpose to cover expected no-shows, with the exact oversell number set by a revenue manager based on cancellation policy and local demand) and more confirmed guests show up than there are rooms, the hotel "walks" the overflow guests to another property. Industry rules say the replacement is supposed to be "comparable" — but one NBC report specifically flags that hotels have quietly been dropping the "comparable" standard in practice, walking guests to lesser properties while still calling it the same thing ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna30269473), [ImmiHelp](https://www.immihelp.com/what-does-it-mean-to-be-walked-by-a-hotel/)). That single term is the hook, and it opens the door to a small glossary delivered the same way: **comp** (complimentary — giving something away free), **attrition** (when a group contract books fewer rooms than it committed to), **FOH/BOH** (front of house vs. back of house — guest-facing vs. staff-only space). **The English-learning layer this project explicitly wants, done the right way**: these are real English words a non-native-English hospitality worker or frequent business traveler would benefit from actually knowing, taught through the insider mechanism rather than as a dry vocabulary list — the exact "editorial-insider framing + practical English value" combination CLAUDE.md names as a proven-strong pattern (same family as "Uncomplimentary Pants," though that one graded a mistranslation and this one explains a term native English speakers in the industry use but guests never hear). **Honest limitation**: sourcing on "walk" and "oversell" is solid and multi-sourced (an industry careers site, a hotel-ops trade explainer, NBC, and a dedicated "walk policy" explainer), but one source notes Hilton itself declined to explain its own walk policy on record, and sources disagree on how often walking happens today (one says less often than in the past) — `story-researcher` should frame frequency claims as "it still happens, and here's the mechanism," not attach a specific rate without a better source. Source: today's pulse-check. Platform(s): YouTube (room to actually walk through the mechanism and the small glossary), XHS (a tighter cut leading with "walked" as the single hook). Asset fit: strong — draws directly on hospitality-management training to explain a real operational term a generalist travel creator wouldn't know to go looking for, and the English-vocabulary layer is the compatible secondary pillar CLAUDE.md calls out, not a bolted-on gimmick. Register: comedic insider. Hook: you've probably been "walked" by a hotel and never knew there was a name for it — and the name is the least embarrassing part of what actually happens next.
