Today it does land: the **Alexander Hamilton Hotel fire in Paterson, New Jersey happened on October 18, 1984** — exactly today's date — and this project's own standing rule (check for a disaster tied to the exact Shanghai calendar date) exists precisely for this case. The fire killed **15 people and injured over 50** ([US Deadly Events](https://www.usdeadlyevents.com/1984-oct-18-arson-fire-alexander-hamilton-residential-hotel-paterson-nj-15/), [Fire Engineering](https://www.fireengineering.com/firefighting/paterson-nj-s-fatal-hotel-fire/)) — a trade-press account at the time called it the **worst hotel disaster in New Jersey's history**. It was arson: a resident, **Russell W. Conklin, 44**, a TV repairman living in the hotel, set the fire; he was convicted of manslaughter and arson and sentenced on November 6, 1985 (released in 1997). One named victim who died of injuries later, **Christino Ramirez, 53**, died October 24 at Hackensack Medical Center's burn unit. **The mechanism that makes this more than a tragedy write-up — and the actual insider-editorial angle**: the fire started on the third floor of the eight-story building and should have been contained there, but investigators found the stairway doors had been left unclosed, and the building's vertical ventilation shafts acted as a chimney, pulling smoke and flame up through four or five of the eight floors into guest rooms far from the original fire ([Fire Engineering](https://www.fireengineering.com/firefighting/paterson-nj-s-fatal-hotel-fire/), [USDeadlyEvents](https://www.usdeadlyevents.com/1984-oct-18-arson-fire-alexander-hamilton-residential-hotel-paterson-nj-15/)). That's not an abstract "fire is bad" story — it's a specific, explainable building-science failure (compartmentalization and self-closing stairway doors exist as a fire-code requirement *because* of exactly this failure mode), which is the kind of mechanism a Swiss-hotel-management-trained host can walk through with real authority: why hotel stairwell doors are designed to close on their own, why "vertical shaft" is a word fire marshals specifically worry about in any multi-story hotel, and why this one still gets cited in fire-safety literature decades later. **Honest limitation, stated rather than papered over:** death-toll figures vary slightly by source (an initial next-day *New York Times* report said 13 dead/57 injured, later revised up to 15); no source found this round ties a specific New Jersey or national fire-code amendment directly to this fire by name — the federal Hotel and Motel Fire Safety Act of 1990 (requiring sprinkler/smoke-detector standards for federally funded travel) postdates it by six years and has documented 1987–89 legislative history, but nothing confirms this exact fire as its direct trigger, so `story-researcher` should not state that causal link on camera without finding a source that makes it explicit. The building was a **residential/SRO-style hotel, not a luxury property** — this is not a brand-collaboration story and the "hotel" here looks nothing like this creator's usual subject matter, which is both the honest limitation and the point: it's a chance to show the editorial range CLAUDE.md's Speeed-inspired model calls for (gripping hotel history doesn't require a five-star address) while staying inside the hotel-history category the creator has already explicitly validated via the Ritz Paris and Manila Hotel entries already in the production queue.
